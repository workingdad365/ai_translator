Harbour city rethinks its night ferries after a winter of complaints

For most of the past decade, the last ferry out of Vellmar Harbour left at ten in the evening, and almost nobody questioned the schedule. It suited the commuters who worked ordinary hours, and it kept fuel costs predictable for the operator. What changed, according to the regional transport board, was not the level of demand but who was travelling, and when.

The review began after residents of the outer islands filed a petition last autumn. Their complaint was blunt: hospital appointments, evening classes, and shift work in the city no longer fit inside a timetable written for office workers. People were hiring private boats, sleeping in waiting rooms, or missing their appointments altogether.

"We kept hearing the same sentence in different words," said Ilse Vantar, who chaired the review. "The service exists, but not when I need it."

Vantar said the board had assumed for years that low passenger counts on late crossings meant low demand. In fact the crossings had been cut back so far that planning around them had become impossible, and the emptiness the board was measuring was largely of its own making.

A quiet problem with counting

The review found that the operator judged success by average occupancy, a figure that rewards removing empty sailings and punishes keeping them. By that measure, a half-empty midnight ferry looks like waste. By another measure — how many islanders can reach the mainland hospital and return the same day — it looks like the one thing holding the network together.

Changing the measure proved harder than changing the timetable. The contract had been written around occupancy targets, and the penalties attached to them were severe enough that no manager would risk a lightly used sailing. The board spent four months renegotiating clauses that passengers will never read, but that had shaped every decision made on their behalf.

What the new schedule actually does

From March, three additional crossings will run between eleven at night and half past one in the morning, on weekdays only. Officials have been careful not to call this a night service. Weekend sailings are unchanged, and the operator has warned that rough weather will suspend the late runs before any others.

Fares will not rise immediately. The extra cost is being absorbed by the regional transport fund for a trial period, after which the board must publish an assessment and decide whether to continue. Critics note that trials of this kind rarely end in cancellation once people have rearranged their lives around them. Supporters reply that this is precisely the point.

Whether the approach travels well is another question. Vellmar's islands sit close together, its population is concentrated near two landing points, and its operator was willing to reopen a contract mid-term. Remove any one of those conditions and the arithmetic falls apart.

What does transfer, Vantar argued, is the habit of asking what a service is for before asking how efficiently it runs. "Efficiency is a good answer to a question," she said. "It is a terrible substitute for one."
